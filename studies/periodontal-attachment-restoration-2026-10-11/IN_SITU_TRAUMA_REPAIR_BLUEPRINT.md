# In-situ tooth-trauma repair: engineering blueprint

## Research decision

The evidence supports a focused engineering program for rebuilding a functional
cementum–periodontal ligament (PDL)–alveolar-bone interface. In-socket
subluxation and intrusive-luxation models do exist, and can measure native
healing or apical tissue responses. I did not identify a qualifying study that
combines a regenerative intervention with baseline-registered tooth position,
load-bearing PDL function, and durability after temporary stabilization is
removed. Most positive attachment-regeneration studies still start with an
extracted root or a surgically created periodontal defect. A 2024 PDLSC-laden
collagen construct added root-fragment surface organization and ectopic
follow-up, but it did not recreate an alveolar socket or physiological tooth
loading. The combined in-socket, load-bearing, post-support durability test
remains the key gap.

Separate two outcomes throughout the program:

1. **Position:** the tooth's three-dimensional location and angulation relative
   to an unaffected pretreatment reference.
2. **Attachment:** a living, vascularized, correctly oriented, load-bearing PDL
   inserted into cementum and alveolar bone, without ankylosis or root
   resorption.

Restoring attachment alone cannot be counted as correction of a tooth that has
already settled into a different position. Position is measured against a
pretreatment image or an anatomically registered contralateral reference.
Temporary stabilization can be standardized during model development, but the
durability endpoint is assessed after it is removed.

Two direct in-socket studies make the transfer gap more specific. In a rat
subluxation model, applying orthodontic tooth movement one or three days after
impact did not improve the histologic quality of periodontal repair relative
to the movement-only comparison; that experiment did not ask whether a tooth
could be guided back to its pretreatment pose or stay there after support
withdrawal. In a separate immature-rat intrusion model, spontaneous re-eruption
occurred during follow-up, while pulp changes and some ankylosis/replacement
resorption were also observed. Position change therefore cannot stand in for
native attachment or pulp recovery. [Busato et al., 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24713565/);
[intrusive-luxation model, 2020](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.joen.2020.07.031).

## What the existing experiments establish

| Strategy | Model and result | What it contributes | What remains unproven |
|---|---|---|---|
| PDL cell sheet | Delayed canine replantation; the study reports favourable histologic healing in 88.4% with multilayer PDL cell sheets versus 5.3% in controls at 8 weeks, with new PDL and cementum. | Cell delivery can improve attachment healing in a severe replantation model. | No regenerative intervention comparison in an in-socket injury model, durable function, or position after fixation withdrawal. [Study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21907971/) |
| Endogenous cell homing | Beagle roots had PDL stripped, were air-dried, decoronated, and replanted; EDTA plus SDF1/BMP7 coating produced complete histologic healing in 5/12 roots at 6 months. The remaining treated roots still showed some ankylosis or resorption. | Local recruitment signals plus root-surface conditioning can produce inserted collagen fibers and vascularized PDL-like tissue without cell expansion. | Three dogs supplied the roots; root-level results are clustered within animals. SDF1, BMP7, EDTA, and release kinetics were not separated; complete healing was below half. [Study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4647325/) |
| In-socket subluxation healing | Ninety Wistar rats received a measured impact injury, with or without traumatic occlusion, and were examined at 7 or 21 days. Added traumatic occlusion delayed repair and altered PDL width, collagen, and bone area. | A direct retained-tooth impact model exists; occlusal loading is a design variable, not a nuisance to ignore. | No regenerative intervention, pretreatment coordinate tracking, long-term mechanics, or post-stabilization position endpoint. [Study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28208234/) |
| Subluxation model calibration | Eighteen adult Wistar rats were assigned to six force groups (n=3); 900 cN produced gingival/periodontal changes judged compatible with subluxation, while 1000 cN caused fractures. | Provides a published force-calibration starting point for a retained-tooth injury model. | Small groups and immediate histology; force is not a transferable dose across devices/species, and no baseline-registered displacement or long-term repair was measured. [Study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20089060/) |
| In-socket subluxation plus induced movement | Forty adult rats were assigned to eight groups; after a 900 cN impact, 50 cN induced tooth movement began at day 1 or 3. Histologic periodontal response in these groups was similar to movement-only animals. | Tests a direct force-after-impact question and cautions against assuming added tooth movement improves periodontal repair. | It did not measure return to pretreatment pose, root-level 3D position, load-bearing recovery, or durability after support removal; n=5/group. [Study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24713565/) |
| Subluxation pulp response | Ninety Wistar rats were assigned to naïve, subluxation, and subluxation-plus-traumatic-occlusion groups and examined at 7 or 21 days. Several pulp changes normalized by day 21, while traumatic occlusion was associated with more pulp calcification and inflammatory changes. | Shows that pulp recovery and PDL repair need separate outcome modules; occlusal loading can alter pulp response as well. | This study focuses on the pulp–dentin complex, not tooth pose or functional PDL restoration. [Study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38420008/) |
| Extrusive-luxation model validation | Forty adult Wistar rats received one of three applied extrusion forces and were examined histologically after 1 or 3 days; PDL bleeding increased with force, and 1500 cN produced the greatest damage. | A distinct model can create acute PDL injury under extrusive loading, making it a candidate for the displaced-tooth track. | Acute histology only; no serial tooth-position measurement, restoration, functional attachment, or post-support durability endpoint. [Study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29214377/) |
| Immature lateral luxation | Three-week-old Wistar rat molars were pushed horizontally toward the palate to a constant dislocation and followed histologically for up to 56 days. | Direction-specific precedent for a tooth shifted sideways within its socket; periodontal, root, and pulp responses were observed over time. | Developing molars; cervical root resorption continued through follow-up; no baseline-registered pose recovery or functional durability. [Study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/1915123/) |
| Lateral luxation with 3D imaging | A related model in 4-week-old rats used 3D micro-CT and histology over 3–4 weeks; root fracture was reported in 4/8 mesial roots and external root resorption in all experimental molars. | Shows 3D imaging can characterize root/socket complications after lateral displacement. | Immature teeth and substantial root complications make it a poor stand-in for uncomplicated mature-tooth drift; no validated serial pose-error or post-support endpoint. [Study](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.pdj.2017.08.001) |
| Immature in-socket intrusion | A 20 N intrusion model in 3-week-old rats tracked micro-CT and histology from 3 to 90 days; spontaneous re-eruption occurred during follow-up. | Supplies a distinct positional natural-history model and shows that eruption/position can change after injury. | Immature molars are not mature adult teeth; re-eruption does not establish native PDL mechanics, and pulp changes, ankylosis, and replacement resorption were also reported. [Study](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.joen.2020.07.031) |
| Human subluxation natural history | A longitudinal permanent-tooth cohort included 404 subluxated teeth; among mature-root teeth, 3-year risks were 3.6% for surface resorption, 0.6% for infection-related resorption, 0.6% for ankylosis-related resorption, and 0.6% for marginal bone loss. No teeth were lost during observation. | Mild in-socket injuries often have a strong natural-healing baseline; any regenerative adjunct must outperform matched standard-care healing. | Observational cohort with clinically managed injuries; it does not establish molecular mechanisms or post-stabilization mechanics. [Study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22804794/) |
| Human extrusive/lateral luxation natural history | A longitudinal cohort followed 82 extrusive and 179 lateral luxations, with scheduled reviews through 10 years. At 3 years in mature-root teeth, surface resorption was 15.6% after extrusion and 29.5% after lateral luxation; ankylosis-related resorption was rare. | Stratify by displacement pattern and root maturity; these injuries have a different natural history from simple subluxation. | Observational clinical outcomes; no regenerative intervention or direct force–displacement recovery endpoint. [Study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22971023/) |
| Pediatric intrusion positional recovery | A clinical series of 170 intruded permanent anterior teeth in children/adolescents associated spontaneous re-eruption with intrusion depth and direction, root development, and injuries to adjacent teeth. | Position can change during natural healing; register tooth position separately from PDL histology and function. | Developing teeth only; no inference to mature adult roots or to functional ligament restoration. [Study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11759786/) |
| Natural repair in immature teeth | A 1986 experimental study in young dogs reported that periodontal fibers after subluxation returned toward their former state by day 20, with no ankylosis; pulp and periodontal vascular/nerve recovery were also described. | Developmental teeth provide a natural-repair benchmark and highlight pulp, nerve, and root-maturity outcomes. | Japanese-language study of immature roots; not an adult regeneration treatment or a positional-stability test under prolonged load. [Study record](https://cir.nii.ac.jp/crid/1390001206346580096) |
| Notch modulation after severe intrusion | A 2026 immature-rat intrusive-luxation study reported that pharmacologic Notch-pathway inhibition reduced aberrant apical fibrosis and increased vascular proliferation. | Apical fibrosis and vascular repair are separable from PDL attachment; the pulp/apex deserves its own outcome module. | Apical tissue signal only; no demonstrated PDL reconstruction, baseline tooth-position recovery, or long-term functional endpoint. Treat as a preclinical hypothesis, not a human treatment. [Study](https://doi.org/10.1590/1678-7765-2025-0490) |
| Fiber-guiding multiphasic scaffold | A CT-designed scaffold was tested in rat periodontal fenestration defects after cementum removal. Oriented PDL-like fibers and attachments to tooth and bone were more consistent with the guided architecture than with an amorphous scaffold; 9/12 guided constructs retained a PDL architecture at 6 weeks. | Geometry and fiber orientation matter alongside cell identity and mineral formation. | This is a surgically opened defect with short follow-up, not restoration of an intact socket after blunt trauma. [Study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4074763/) |
| Layered BMP carrier | In a monkey furcation-defect study, a double-layer collagen-membrane approach was reported to regenerate cementum, ligament, and alveolar process with perpendicular collagen fibers and no ankylosis at 12 weeks; the single-layer approach often ankylosed. | Spatially separating regenerative compartments may reduce bone bridging across the ligament space. | Older defect-model result; no in-socket trauma, long-term positional test, or current manufacturing specification. [Study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9541226/) |
| Human PDL–bone microtissue | A 3D-bioprinted, two-cell human model maintained shape under microfluidic flow for more than 10 days, with cell viability beyond 7 days and PDL-fibroblast/osteoblast layer cohesion and migration. | A human-cell interface can support early material, perfusion, and dose-screening work. | It does not yet recreate a cementum-facing root, aligned inserting fibers, mature neurovascularized ligament, or tooth-scale mechanical function. [Study](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.gendis.2020.11.011) |
| Root-surface PDLSC collagen construct | A 2024 study printed PDLSC-laden collagen on excised root fragments; in vitro, cells migrated and aligned at the surface. Ectopic root-fragment implants in immunocompromised mice were examined at 4 and 10 weeks. The authors report no new cementum in the in-vitro root-surface experiment and describe the parallel cell alignment as short of functionally oriented PDL insertion. | The closest recent root-facing cell-placement benchmark; tests surface coverage, migration, viability, and alignment on a root substrate. | Ectopic root-fragment implantation has no alveolar socket or occlusal loading and does not establish insertion at both interfaces, tooth position, or functional attachment. [Study](https://doi.org/10.1021/acsami.4c13830) |

These are preclinical feasibility signals across different injury models and
endpoints. Their percentages must not be pooled or described as human success
rates.

## Model selection for impact-related loosening and displacement

Choose the model by the injury phenotype and keep the inference bounded:

| Target injury | Best current model family | What it can answer now | Required additions before it answers restoration |
|---|---|---|---|
| A loosened tooth that remains in its socket | Adult retained-tooth subluxation, with force calibration and a no-added-occlusal-trauma arm | Whether an intervention changes repair of native-root PDL after impact | Same-modality baseline and follow-up tooth/root pose, attachment and pulp outcomes, mechanics, plus durability after temporary support withdrawal |
| A tooth shifted or tilted within its socket | Lateral luxation is the closest direction-specific precedent, but published rat models use immature molars; adult-rat extrusion is a different-direction candidate, and intrusion is a separate natural-history case | Sideways displacement injury, acute extrusive PDL injury, or intrusion-related positional change, each within its own model | Develop a mature-root-relevant lateral-displacement model; quantify actual displacement, register six-degree-of-freedom pose, and measure PDL function and post-support stability over longer follow-up |
| Severe loss of viable root-surface PDL | Delayed replantation / stripped-root model | Whether a root-surface cell or homing strategy can rebuild attachment under severe injury | Keep as an attachment positive control; do not use its results as evidence for in-socket pose restoration |

The 2010 subluxation calibration study is only a starting point: it used three
animals per force level and immediate histology. Its reported force should not
be copied as a dose for another apparatus or species. For a tooth shifted
sideways, the nearest rat precedents deliberately produce lateral displacement,
but in immature teeth; a later 3D-CT study also reported root fractures and
external resorption. The adult-rat extrusion study adds a different
displacement-direction candidate, but its 1- and 3-day histology does not
establish return to original pose. The 2024 90-rat study is a pulp–dentin
response study, while the similar-sized 2017 study supplies periodontal repair
outcomes; these complement one another but must not be conflated.
[Subluxation calibration, 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20089060/);
[periodontal repair, 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28208234/);
[pulp response, 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38420008/);
[extrusive model, 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29214377/);
[immature lateral luxation](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/1915123/);
[3D-CT luxation model](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.pdj.2017.08.001).

## Consequence for the no-permanent-retention goal

The target should be a tooth that remains near its declared original pose after
temporary positional support is removed because its own attachment and
remodeling systems function again. The literature reviewed here does not show
that a regenerative cue alone can return a displaced, in-socket tooth to that
pose. A position guide and a biological repair strategy are separate factors:
the former manages geometry during healing, while the latter must restore
cementum–PDL–bone structure and function. A candidate should be compared with
the same position-management conditions and judged after support withdrawal;
otherwise an alignment effect can be mistaken for regenerated attachment.
The rat movement-after-subluxation result is a reason to measure this directly,
not evidence that all positional guidance fails. No lifelong retainer is a
preclinical durability endpoint here, not a promise about an available human
treatment.

Human trauma cohorts add a separate natural-history benchmark: mild subluxation
has a low rate of severe periodontal complications, while displaced extrusion
and lateral luxation carry higher surface-resorption risks, particularly in
mature-root teeth. Pediatric intrusion cohorts also show that position may
change spontaneously and depends on injury geometry and tooth development.
These cohorts support injury-stratified controls and coordinate tracking; they
do not demonstrate a rejuvenating or regenerative adjunct.

## Staged engineering program

### Gate 1 — Lock the native restoration contract

Use the existing [restoration contract](../restoration-run-plan-2026-10-11/PLAN.md)
and add four gates before any intervention is ranked:

- **Interface:** continuous cementum-facing and bone-facing attachment, with
  collagen bundles inserted at both mineralized boundaries and a maintained
  ligament space.
- **Mechanics:** force–displacement response, hysteresis/energy dissipation,
  stress relaxation, and recovery after cyclic load compared with matched
  healthy tissue. A stiff response alone can be ankylosis.
- **Safety:** quantify ankylosis, inflammatory and replacement root resorption,
  pulp status, vascularity, inflammation, and ectopic mineralization separately.
- **Position and durability:** track tooth position before injury, during
  repair, and after temporary fixation removal; test for drift under repeated
  physiologic loading over months.

Histology, transcript markers, bone volume, or a single pull-out value cannot
substitute for this combined contract.

### Gate 2 — Build a human-cell interface model

Use the published root-surface PDLSC-collagen construct as the root-facing
organization benchmark and the human PDL–bone microtissue as a compartment and
perfusion benchmark. Add the missing tooth-support features in stages: a
defined ligament-width region, a root/cementum-facing mineral surface, a
separate alveolar-bone-facing compartment, and anisotropic fibers that insert
at both boundaries. Keep PDL fibroblasts/PDL progenitors and osteogenic cells
spatially distinct; add perfused endothelial support only after the static
two-interface structure is reproducible. Keep gingival epithelium separate.
The detailed organoid-workbench assay contract is in
[`studies/organoid/periodontal-ligament-interface/`](../organoid/periodontal-ligament-interface/README.md).

First qualify the root-facing benchmark, then compare biphasic versus
homogeneous architecture, then aligned versus non-aligned guidance. Add flow
and cyclic loading only after architecture and measurement repeatability pass.
Cell-sheet delivery and endogenous-cell-homing signals remain separate future
factor comparisons. Measure cell identity, fiber orientation and insertion at
each interface, mineral deposition location, viability, inflammation,
leachables, and low-amplitude cyclic mechanics. Include a bone-bridge/ankylosis
positive-control signature so the assay rejects a construct that is strong
only because it is too stiff. Freeze numeric acceptance limits only after
repeatability and matched-reference distributions are measured.

### Gate 3 — Separate injury models instead of extrapolating between them

Maintain three model tracks with separate conclusions:

1. **Retained-tooth subluxation / stretch:** adapt the published measured-impact
   model while preserving the native root surface. Add baseline-registered
   tooth coordinates, PDL imaging, and follow-up after any temporary support is
   removed. This directly addresses loosening after impact; it does not by
   itself model a tooth that has shifted to a new angle.
2. **Retained-tooth displacement:** prioritize a lateral-luxation model for
   tilted/side-shifted teeth, but first address the immature-root limitation in
   published rat models. Keep extrusion and intrusion as separate directional
   injury tracks. Track position separately from attachment and distinguish
   apical pulp repair from periodontal repair.
3. **Root-surface injury / delayed replantation:** use as a severe attachment
   loss positive-control model for cell sheets or homing cues, not as a proxy
   for an in-socket tooth.
4. **Periodontal defect:** use to benchmark fiber-guiding and multiphasic
   scaffolds where bone and cementum must be rebuilt around a defect.

Before animal testing, validate measurement repeatability in an ex-vivo
tooth–socket loading setup and the human-cell interface model. For any later
animal study, predefine the biological unit, randomize at the animal level,
blind imaging and histology, account for multiple teeth nested within each
animal, and use a time course long enough to distinguish transient attachment
from stable repair. Preserve an untreated injury arm and an established
healing-care comparator; do not define success by reduced mobility alone.

### Gate 4 — Make the decisive durability test

The decisive test begins only after a candidate passes interface, mechanics,
and safety gates. Record tooth coordinates and socket geometry at baseline,
after injury, and after recovery. Remove temporary stabilization at a
prespecified point, then assess position, physiologic mobility, repeated-load
response, periodontal space, fiber insertion, root integrity, pulp vitality,
and inflammation at short and long follow-up. A repair that needs continuing
external force to hold alignment has not met the requested restoration target.

   root complications. None supplies baseline-registered pose recovery or
   post-support durability. Keep intrusion as a separate developmental
   natural-history track. The 2014 post-subluxation movement study did not
   improve histologic repair over movement-only controls. Do not silently
   substitute replantation outcomes for in-socket restoration.
4. Pre-register the minimum animal-level design only after the ex-vivo assay
   demonstrates repeatable mechanical and ankylosis discrimination.

No human treatment protocol is implied by this research blueprint. Acute
trauma care and research-grade regenerative reconstruction are separate
questions; this program evaluates models and evidence rather than directing
care.
